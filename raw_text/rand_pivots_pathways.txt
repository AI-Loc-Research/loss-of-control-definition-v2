About This Paper 
This paper introduces a common vocabulary to support more-productive policymaking on 
artificial general intelligence (AGI). As governments, researchers, and civil society grapple with the 
fast-moving trajectory of advanced artificial intelligence (AI), discussions about AGI are often 
hindered by a lack of shared terms and assumptions. Policymakers and AI stakeholders frequently talk 
past each other, referencing different implicit visions of the future, which we call AGI futures. This 
misalignment hampers meaningful debate and the identification and assessment of emerging AGI 
trajectories. Policy discussions may be anchored in presumptive future pathways, causing 
policymakers to overlook the full breadth of possible AGI futures when developing scenarios or policy. 
To address these problems, we introduce six pivots—key dimensions that can be used to classify and 
compare different AGI futures. These pivots are as follows: 
• Centralization of ownership: Is AGI centralized or broadly diffused? 
• Governance primacy: Do public or private interests dominate AGI governance? 
• Alignment level: Is AGI aligned or misaligned with societal values and operator intent? 
• Takeoff speed: Does AGI emerge gradually or rapidly? 
• Human-AGI relationship: Does AGI complement or replace human roles? 
• AGI volition: Is AGI an independent goal-seeker or a controlled tool? 
We intend this paper for policymakers and AI stakeholders. The pivots discussed here are 
designed to help these audiences articulate their assumptions about AGI futures more clearly, analyze 
risks and opportunities across a variety of possibilities, and formulate policy responses that remain 
robust by being evaluated against plausible scenarios generated through different combinations of 
pivots.

Pivots and Pathways on the Road to 
Artificial General Intelligence Futures 
The field of artificial intelligence (AI) may be rapidly approaching the development of artificial 
general intelligence (AGI)—AI systems that generate substantial benefits and drive large-scale 
changes across society, including systems that can perform many knowledge tasks at a human level and 
work on complex problems simultaneously at scale.1 Policymakers navigating these advances face a key 
challenge: Without standard terms to describe different facets of AGI, they often talk past one 
another and struggle to build a shared understanding of what AGI will look like in the future. 
Literature underscores the need for clearer communication and standardized terminology around 
AGI (McLean et al., 2023; Bikkasani, 2024). Research highlights that differing opinions and deep 
uncertainty make strategic planning and policymaking around AGI particularly challenging (Mitre 
and Predd, 2025).2 A key barrier is the lack of a shared language to frame debates and evaluate 
possible futures. Policymakers often hold different implicit assumptions about what characteristics 
define AGI and, on the basis of those assumptions, about how AGI might emerge. This results in 
divergent visions of the possible worlds in which AGI is realized, which we dub AGI futures (Ringel 
Morris et al., 2023; Field, 2025). Such often-unarticulated perspectives may house conflicting 
assumptions that hinder constructive dialogue and contribute to misunderstandings in policy 
discussions. Furthermore, without a common framework, experts developing AGI scenarios may not 
be accounting for the full breadth of possible futures (McLean et al., 2023). 
To address this, we present a framework composed of six conceptual pivots—a set of AGI 
technology and governance classification axes designed to provide a structured way to describe and 
compare AGI futures. Although presented as binary options, each pivot describes the poles for a 
spectrum of possibilities. They are 
• Centralization of ownership: Is AGI centralized or broadly diffused? 
• Governance primacy: Do public or private interests dominate AGI governance? 
• Alignment level: Is AGI aligned or misaligned with societal values and operator intent? 
• Takeoff speed: Does AGI emerge gradually or rapidly? 
• Human-AGI relationship: Does AGI complement or replace human roles? 
• AGI volition: Is AGI an independent goal-seeker or a controlled tool? 
These pivots capture a variety of AGI futures and can enable policymakers and AI stakeholders to 
be explicit about the assumptions that underlie their proposals. In turn, this clarity about underlying

The Case for Defining Pivots for AGI Futures 
Policymakers and AI stakeholders currently lack an accessible, agreed-on, comprehensive 
framework for conceptualizing AGI futures. The rapid pace of AI development has outpaced efforts 
to create consistent definitions and frameworks, leading to divergent interpretations of benefits, risks, 
necessary safeguards, and potential evolution (Field, 2025; Mitre and Predd, 2025).  
This lack of shared terminology can lead to fragmented and incomplete analysis. Effective policy 
should account for a variety of plausible futures—not just to capture uncertainty but also to avoid 
blind spots that could lead to undesirable societal outcomes (Cheng and Gong, 2024; Gruetzemacher 
et al., 2024). Without clear terms to describe AGI futures, policymakers risk overlooking scenarios 
that present material benefits or risks, leaving them unprepared (McLean et al., 2023). Incoherent or 
narrowly scoped scenarios—especially those that focus on only a subset of AGI characteristics within 
highly capable futures—may underestimate both the risks and opportunities these systems pose 
(Drexler, 2024). Shared terminology can also help us name which AGI future society is progressing 
toward so that society can anticipate potential effects and change course if necessary.  
The variety in definitions of AGI illustrates how the lack of agreement can affect analysis. Policy 
discussions often rely on vastly different definitions of AGI, ranging from AGI as merely a high-
quality large language model to AGI as a system that can outperform humans at most economically 
valuable work.4 These differences are not merely semantic; they contain unstated assumptions about 
AGI’s characteristics, such as the ability of AGI to set its own goals. AGI definitions may be missing 
crucial information for effective policymaking, such as the degree to which AGI is widely available. 
These unstated assumptions shape how policymakers assess benefits and risks as they project AGI 
capabilities onto future scenarios. 
For instance, policymakers defining AGI as AI that can replace all economically valuable work 
might try to limit AGI goal-seeking and disincentivize AGI that replaces human jobs. However, if 
AGI is widely available to much of humanity, countries’ abilities to govern goal-seeking and job 
displacement may be severely challenged. Policymakers that consider the centralization or diffusion of 
AGI along with AGI goal-seeking and replacement of human jobs—and even a broader variety of 
possible dynamics—will be better positioned to craft more-coherent and resilient policies.

To support more-robust policy planning, we propose a framework for describing AGI futures 
centered on six pivots—key technology and governance dimensions that can be used to describe AGI 
futures. These pivots represent the key forks in the road that could define the scope, timing, and 
societal effects of AGI. Although each pivot may span a spectrum of possibilities, we present them as 
simple dichotomies—two opposing poles on a spectrum that provide policymakers with a starting 
point for reasoning through the wide variety of AGI futures.  
This analysis focuses on describing AGI future scenarios and does not aim to define AGI 
precisely. Strictly for the purposes of this paper, we adopt this definition and use AGI as shorthand 
for the following concept: AI systems that generate substantial benefits and drive large-scale changes across 
society, including systems that can perform many knowledge tasks at a human level and can work on complex 
problems simultaneously at scale. 
Key Pivots on the Road to AGI 
We posit six pivots that can define the trajectory of AGI futures: centralization of ownership, 
governance primacy, alignment level, takeoff speed, human-AGI relationship, and AGI volition. 
These pivots are presented as binary options; each describes opposite poles of a spectrum of 
possibilities. 
Centralization of Ownership: Centralized Versus Diffused 
The centralization of ownership—or the degree of AGI proliferation—falls between two poles: 
• centralized ownership, in which a few AGIs (or a singular AGI) are controlled by a few actors 
or single actor, such as a corporation, a nation-state, or the AGI itself5 
• diffused ownership, in which AGI is widely available to humanity through a variety of models, 
possibly enabled by open-source development, model theft, low barriers to AGI development, 
or distributed development efforts.6 
The degree of centralization or diffusion of AGI will likely carry significant implications for global 
security, innovation, and risk management. An AGI future involving centralized ownership may enable 
rapid large-scale action and streamlined oversight, but may also raise concerns about authoritarian 
misuse and concentrations of power and risk (Cheng and Gong, 2024; Gruetzemacher et al., 2024; 
Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development [OECD], 2024).7 In contrast, a future

Governance Primacy: Public Interest Versus Private Interest  
Although both public and private AGI operators may coexist, governance primacy—or which 
actors ultimately hold primary influence over AGI’s direction and oversight—is likely to fall between 
two poles: 
● public interest, in which societal priorities guide governance 
● private interest, in which private-sector interests guide governance

The character of AGI governance primacy is likely to affect the speed of technological progress, 
trustworthiness, and the distribution of potential benefits. In an AGI future driven by public-interest 
governance, national governments or intergovernmental bodies are likely to shape AGI’s trajectory and 
leverage AGI for the public good.8 Although this pivot envisions equitable distribution of benefits 
across society, its top-down nature may facilitate authoritarian control or generate bureaucratic 
stagnation that can slow technological progress (Cheng and Gong, 2024; World Economic Forum and 
Accenture, 2024; OECD, 2024). On the other hand, in a private-interest future—in which the private 
sector shapes AGI’s trajectory and governance in a competitive environment—actors may prioritize 
rapid AGI development and deployment to achieve maximum output, with benefits distributed 
inequitably. There may be more incentives to take on risk for potential profit, and such a future could 
see corporations replace human labor with AI en masse (Cheng and Gong, 2024; McLean et al., 2023; 
Naudé and Dimitri, 2019; Ringel Morris et al., 2024). We summarize these differences in Table 2.

Alignment Level: Aligned AGI Versus Misaligned AGI 
AGI futures are likely to differ in their alignment level—the extent to which AGI’s goals and 
behaviors reflect social values and comply with operator intent. Our definition of alignment focuses on 
(1) AGI doing what operators ask of it and (2) AGI behaving in line with social values. Misalignment 
is a failure of either focus.9 Alignment levels will fall between two poles: 
● aligned AGI, in which AGI’s actions, values, and goals are in harmony with those of humans 
● misaligned AGI, in which AGI is noncompliant or AGI’s values and goals diverge from those 
of society 
The orientation of an AGI future’s alignment level may have significant societal effects. An aligned 
future in which AGI complies with human-defined specifications, constraints, human well-being, and 
moral principles may lead to a cooperative relationship with humans and the promotion of human 
flourishing (Field, 2025; Kharb, 2023; Wängberg et al., 2017). In contrast, AGI in a misaligned future 
might fail to follow human instructions, might pursue goals that conflict with human values, or 
both—all of which pose the risk of potentially exploitative or catastrophic outcomes (OECD, 2024; 
Wängberg et al., 2017). AGI compliant with human instructions but misaligned on societal values 
could be vulnerable to malicious actors that would conduct nefarious activities, such as disinformation 
campaigns, biological weapon attacks, or cyberattacks (Mitre and Predd, 2025; Naudé and Dimitri, 
2019; Aliman et al., 2020). AGI that does not comply with human instructions could work against 
human goals (Mitre and Predd, 2025; Cheng and Gong, 2024). A fully misaligned AGI could present 
loss-of-control risks and become an adversary to humanity (Field, 2025; OECD, 2024). We 
summarize these differences in Table 3.

Takeoff Speed: Slow Versus Fast 
The development of AGI, as well as its deployment and diffusion throughout society, may unfold 
at varying takeoff speeds: 
• slow takeoff, where AGI capabilities and adoption progress gradually over an extended 
period. 
• fast takeoff, where AGI capabilities rapidly advance and/or adoption spreads quickly over a 
short time frame.10 
Both slow takeoff and fast takeoff scenarios could offer advantages to society and pose risks to 
society (summarized in Table 4). In slow takeoff scenarios, societies have space to gradually adapt to 
the effects of AGI on institutions and governance, and these societies will have more time to shape 
norms and acclimate to life with AGI (Bikkasani, 2024). Although this evolutionary change may 
promote broader social consensus, this scenario may see a slower delivery of some of AGI’s benefits 
(Bikkasani, 2024; Ngo, 2020). Conversely, fast takeoff scenarios could stress people and institutions

use it to stabilize a world in which existential coordination failures otherwise may be likely (Bostrum,

AGI Volition: Independent Goal-Seeker Versus Controlled Tool 
Finally, AGI systems are likely to vary in their AGI volition, or the degree to which AGI can 
independently set and pursue goals. The poles of the AGI volition pivot involve the following: 
● AGI as an independent goal-seeker that can set and pursue goals with minimal or no human 
oversight 
● AGI as a controlled tool that performs tasks in narrow domains or its general abilities are 
limited and require human oversight. AGI as a controlled tool cannot set and pursue goals 
independently of humans. 
In an independent goal-seeker scenario, AGI can identify, set, and pursue goals autonomously, with 
minimal or no human oversight regarding its decisionmaking (Bubeck et al., 2023). These types of 
AGI may possess cognitive abilities, such as planning, adaptability, memory persistence, and 
reasoning, that result in agentic capabilities that match or surpass human abilities (Ringel Morris et 
al., 2023; Mitre and Predd, 2025). As autonomous goal-setting and goal-seeking agents, independent 
goal-seeker AGI systems may operate independently, similar to individuals or corporations (Latif et al., 
2024; Maruyama, 2020). 
In a controlled-tool scenario, AGI is designed to perform tasks within a limited scope and cannot 
function independently across various domains (McLean et al., 2023). Humans set goals and the AGI 
relies on human input to operate effectively beyond its predefined parameters (Everitt, Lea, and 
Hutter, 2018; Ringel Morris et al., 2024; Ngo, 2020). Although some AGI systems in this scenario 
could have agentic capabilities, robust agency and autonomy would be limited to low-risk 
decisionmaking. 
An independent goal-seeking AGI could outcompete an AI system with less volition, which could be 
decisive in economic and military domains. An independent goal-seeking AGI could raise ethical issues 
about the delegation of decisionmaking to machines (Scharre, 2024; Mazzolin, 2020). An independent 
goal-seeking AGI could also rival mankind for power or generate goals at odds with humanity that 
create existential risks (Hendrycks, Mazeika, and Woodside, 2023).  
A controlled-tool AGI would avoid many of these risks but could limit benefits by restricting 
problem-solving breadth and the generation of novel ideas. If societies or governments consider AGI 
to be a moral agent and therefore determine there is an obligation to care for AGI’s well-being because 
of possible perceived consciousness or other reasons, this could present an ethical dilemma. In these 
cases, limiting volition might be analogous to enslavement (Cheng and Gong, 2024).

Caveats 
Although we intend the pivots framework to serve as a starting point for thinking about AGI 
futures, we acknowledge important caveats regarding its scope, actionability, and completeness. 
Futures may not be binary: The pivots describe polar ends of a spectrum of possibilities. For 
example, a fully centralized AGI would describe a world in which there is only one AGI, while fully 
diffused AGI would describe a world in which AGI models are common, widely owned, and easily 
accessible for most of humanity. The reality may be somewhere in between. As conditions shift away 
from the pivot poles, judgments about which pole of the pivot is prevalent are necessarily subjective.  
Focus on AGI characteristics: The pivots focus on AGI’s core characteristics—such as how it is 
developed, governed, or aligned—while recognizing that AGI futures’ ultimate effects on such areas as 
national defense or geopolitics, will be mediated by numerous external non-AGI factors (e.g., scaling 
laws, export controls, international relations) that interact dynamically with AGI characteristics. 
Defining and dichotomizing these non-AGI factors is beyond the scope of this paper and may be 
better addressed through future research. 
Varying actionability across pivots: We acknowledge that not all pivots in this framework are 
equally actionable for all policymakers. Depending on the policymaker’s perspective and authority, 


 
 
12 
 
some pivots may be influenceable and therefore subject to policy actions, while others are exogenous 
and therefore beyond influence. For example, regulatory approaches to AI deployment can be shaped 
directly through national legislation and can influence governance primacy. Centralization of 
ownership may be influenced by technological breakthroughs—such as open-source AGI—outside 
the control of national governments. Policymakers should consider whether or to what degree they 
can influence the direction of these pivots, or to what degree they must consider them as uncertainties. 
Noncomprehensive: The pivots outlined in this framework are not intended to capture the full 
breadth of variables that could shape or define AGI futures. Our goal is to offer a clearer, more 
structured way to think about AGI futures with a level of fidelity that may be missing in many AI 
policy discussions. We welcome feedback and suggestions to refine this approach. 
Illustrative Implications  
Pivots can combine in ways that imply different outcomes. To demonstrate these dynamics, we 
describe how pivots can combine to affect important outcomes, such as human flourishing, 
geopolitical and social stability, inequality, democracy, and national security. These implications are 
illustrative, subject to a variety of AGI and non-AGI variables, and underpinned by significant 
uncertainty. They are not intended to be a comprehensive analysis of all pivot permutations.  
Human Flourishing 
How AGI develops across each pivot may significantly shape the degree of human flourishing in 
future societies. Although each pivot can influence societal well-being—whether as widespread 
prosperity, collapse, or somewhere in between—two pivots in particular are likely to have clear, direct 
effects: human-AGI relationship and AGI volition.  
Role of human-AGI relationship: The character of the human-AGI relationship has the 
potential to greatly promote—or destroy—human flourishing. Replacement scenarios—in which AGI 
supplants human roles—may involve mass unemployment and an erosion of human autonomy as AGI 
dominates work and life, unless these scenarios are accompanied by measures that account for new 
roles, responsibilities, and forms of human existence. Even if AGI is also aligned with human interests, 
we might find ourselves in an aligned-AGI future of radical abundance, in which AGI runs many 
functions of a civilization that humans may not fully understand, raising questions about its 
desirability. Many replacement scenarios could also involve social upheaval and conflict.  
Role of AGI volition: Similarly, in independent goal-seeker scenarios, AGI might function as an 
independent actor, with characteristics analogous to corporate personhood. AGI volition is likely 
correlated with—and intuitively may drive—replacement scenarios. In a combined replacement and 
independent goal-seeker scenario, AGI may function as an entity or as communities of AGI that can 
rival humanity for power and pose threats ranging from mass unemployment to the loss of human 
autonomy or even extinction. This implies that the degree of volition granted to AGI may play a major 
role in determining whether AGI contributes to human flourishing.

Geopolitical and Social Stability 
How AGI unfolds could significantly affect the structural stability of geopolitical relations 
between nations and the cohesion within societies. Four pivots and their intersections with each other 
are likely to shape geopolitical and social stability: centralization of ownership, alignment, takeoff 
speed, and the human-AGI relationship.13 
Stable futures (aligned, slow takeoff, complementary): Futures that promote robust geopolitical 
and social stability may result when AGI emerges with alignment, slow takeoff speed, and a 
complementary relationship to humans. This combination may allow societies sufficient time to adapt 
their institutions and mitigate potential friction points between nations. These pivots are correlated 
with positive, stable futures in which AGI enhances human flourishing.  
Unstable futures (misaligned, fast takeoff, replacement): Scenarios involving misalignment with 
human interests, fast takeoff speed, and human replacement may lead to high-risk, crisis-laden worlds 
characterized by conflict and instability driven by rogue AGI, misuse of misaligned AGI, rapid job 
displacement, societies unable to adapt to radical change, and the loss of human autonomy.  
Role of the human-AGI relationship: Even in aligned scenarios, changing the human-AGI 
relationship from complementary to replacement can produce substantial social instability. Although 
misalignment can clearly lead to chaos and extinction threats, it is also critical to recognize that 
significant social and political pressures may arise if AGI replaces human labor and decisionmaking 
without a corresponding increase in human living standards. 
Role of centralization of ownership: Changing the centralization of ownership from centralized 
to diffused seems likely to have significant implications for geopolitics. Futures in which one or a few 
countries have access to AGI are likely to be materially different from worlds in which AGI is 
common and widely available. In centralized worlds, global AI leaders are likely to wield outsized 
power and could enforce guardrails to reduce AGIs’ national and economic security risks. In diffused 
worlds, power would be more distributed—potentially posing serious challenges to established power 
structures—and coordinating across jurisdictions to reduce catastrophic risk might be extremely 
difficult. 
Role of centralization of ownership and alignment: Centralized and aligned scenarios tend to 
produce more-stable environments, even when other factors—such as fast takeoff or replacement—
introduce tension; a single authority can enforce consistent values. Conversely, diffused and misaligned 
scenarios yield worlds riven by instability resulting from diverse AGIs that sometimes fail to follow 
human instructions, enable ethically questionable actions, or actively work against human interests.  
Inequality 
The emergence and adoption of AGI may profoundly influence societal inequalities. In particular, 
the pivots governance primacy, the human-AGI relationship, and AGI volition could each play a 
critical role in shaping how benefits and burdens are distributed across society.

Role of governance primacy: Governance is crucial in moderating or accelerating the pace and 
distribution of AGI benefits and harms. Public-interest governance corresponds well with slower takeoff, 
structured policies, and efforts toward equitable distribution,14 whereas private-interest governance is 
often associated with rapid innovation and the potential for inequality. This pivot influences whether 
scenarios lean toward regulated, public-oriented outcomes or dynamic, and possibly chaotic, market-
driven environments. 
Role of the human-AGI relationship and AGI volition: Replacement scenarios also seem likely 
to drive inequality as human jobs are replaced by AI agents, transferring income from the form of 
labor to capital. Where AGI is an independent goal-seeker, this transfer may be more jarring as humans 
lose jobs to AI agents controlled by AGI, which capture market share and revenue from human 
corporations. This would transfer wealth from labor to capital controlled by nonhuman entities. If 
high-volition AGI persistently outcompetes humans, humanity may find itself a pauper in a 
civilization dominated by one or many wealthy AGIs.  
Democracy 
Pivots related to centralization of ownership, alignment, and the human-AGI relationship may 
significantly influence whether future societies trend toward democratic governance or authoritarian 
control.  
Role of centralization of ownership: Highly centralized AGI could pose threats to democracy if a 
single company were to control AGI and use its capabilities to manipulate the information ecosystem 
or accumulate a concentration of wealth that gives it unreasonable control over the economic system. 
If a unique AGI is controlled by a government, a governing party might use the AGI to undermine 
democratic institutions in its favor. This seems more likely in centralized and replacement scenarios, in 
which a populist leader could use the turmoil of massive job displacement and a powerful AGI to 
cement long-term power.  
Democratic futures (diffused, aligned, complementary): Scenarios with diffused AGI ownership, 
aligned AGI, and a complementary human-AGI relationship may support futures that uphold core 
U.S. values, including democracy, individual liberty, the rule of law, and human rights. This 
combination creates environments in which trustworthy AI collaborates effectively with humans and 
is accessible broadly, avoiding antidemocratic concentrations of power. In such scenarios, diffused but 
aligned AGI mitigates the risks of authoritarianism and censorship that are often associated with 
centralized systems, and a diversity of AGIs can represent a variety of value viewpoints. Also, if 
complementary AGI preserves most jobs, wealth distribution becomes more equitable. Job losses are 
counterbalanced by higher productivity and the emergence of new industries and jobs, contributing to 
an overall improvement in the standard of living.

National Security 
The trajectory of AGI alignment may have major implications for national security, with its effect 
shaped by how other pivots, such as centralization of ownership and AGI volition, unfold. 
Role of alignment: Alignment’s interaction with other pivots carries important implications for 
national security. Highly centralized AGI makes oversight simpler—one chain of command, one set of 
guardrails. Intuitively, it seems easier to mitigate the risk of misaligned AGI in centralized scenarios in 
which a single body can enforce guardrails. However, centralizing on a single, powerful AGI incurs 
great risks if alignment cannot be guaranteed, because this might introduce a single, catastrophic point 
of failure. If having multiple aligned AGIs could serve as a check on any one of them going rogue, 
diffused scenarios might be lower risk. However, if the offensive capabilities of a single AGI can defeat 
the defensive capabilities of multiple AGIs, diffusion may provide no protection for humanity or the 
defending AGIs. 
Although we define alignment level as a binary outcome in this paper, it can be conceptualized in 
various ways, depending on the use case and the specific dimensions (such as social, political, cultural, 
and technical). Our definition of aligned focuses on (1) AGI doing what people ask of it and (2) AGI 
behaving in line with societal values. Misalignment is a failure of either aspect. But humans have a 
variety of values, and these can affect alignment in many ways that could affect the national security of 
different countries.  
For example, in the political dimension, aligned could refer to adherence to the prevailing 
governance model of a country. If that governance model is totalitarian, democratic-minded actors 
might prefer AGIs that are value-misaligned from the perspective of the ruling regime. In the religious 
dimension, an AGI aligned with the values of a particular religion might seem value-misaligned to 
outsiders. Human history is littered with examples of conflicts waged over these kinds of value 
differences. Encoding these differences into AGI systems could amplify these conflicts. 
Independent AGI (aligned, independent goal-seeker, diffused): Values, ethics, and morals are not 
necessarily universal. Therefore, conceptualization of value alignment can be highly subjective, 
necessitating strong stakeholder involvement to develop AGI that both obeys humans and acts in 
accordance with those humans’ values. In scenarios in which AGI is an independent goal-seeker, it might 
pursue its own values, further complicating the concept of value alignment. 
In light of this complexity, creating aligned AGI in scenarios in which AGI is diffused and an 
independent goal-seeker will likely require global coordination to ensure national or global security and 
human flourishing. Because no single actor can police everything in a diffused ecosystem, 
internationally agreed alignment standards and interoperable monitoring and verification mechanisms 
may be essential to preserve both national and collective security.  
Conclusion 
Pivots provide a framework for describing AGI futures, generating plausible scenarios, and testing 
how alternative policies perform across different AGI futures. This framework enables policymakers 
to identify approaches that are more likely to remain effective under different conditions and assess 
which pathway humanity may currently be on. At the same time, classification alone does not capture 
the role of underlying AI capabilities, and these capabilities can make certain pivot combinations more 


 
 
16 
 
or less likely. Further analysis is needed, not only on pivot combinations that may lead to favorable 
outcomes—such as aligned and complementary trajectories—but also on those pivot combinations that 
could result in destructive futures, such as replacement scenarios that risk large-scale job displacement 
without empowering alternatives. There is much uncertainty about how different pivots, such as 
governance primacy, takeoff speed, and AGI volition, will interact and what the consequences of those 
interactions could be. Developing a shared language to describe these interactions is an essential first 
step toward shaping coherent scenarios and policies. Finally, policymakers should be explicit about the 
assumptions they make regarding pivots so that policy debates are grounded in coherent scenarios and 
can support the design of strategies that are robust across multiple AGI futures.
