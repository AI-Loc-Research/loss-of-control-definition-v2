As artificial intelligence (AI) systems become increasingly capable and diffused throughout the economy and

The Case for AI Loss of Control Response Planning 
and an Outline to Get Started 
As artificial intelligence (AI) systems become increasingly capable and diffused throughout the economy and 
society, the possibility of loss of control (LOC) incidents—in which AI systems operate outside meaningful 
human control—may require new national response capabilities, preparedness, and planning. 
Everyday interactions with AI systems have already shown concerning behavior, such as when language 
models hallucinate false information or autonomous vehicles make unexpected driving decisions.1 Experi-
ments in controlled conditions increasingly find that advanced AI models demonstrate behavior that could 
evade human control, including deceiving or blackmailing users to prevent themselves from being replaced 
or shut down.2 Although these real-world incidents and experimental findings may be detected and quickly 
remediated, there is potential for LOC incidents to escalate to greater levels of harm, particularly if they occur 
in high-stakes domains or if human oversight is too slow or insufficient before serious consequences unfold.
Detecting and responding to a significant LOC incident requires not only a technical understanding 
of AI models, but broader whole-of-society planning and coordination with stakeholders in government, 
across companies in the AI tech stack,3 with critical service providers, and with end users. However, the 
existing response environment is underdeveloped, with limited agreement on risks and few coordination 
mechanisms. In that context, the 2025 White House AI Action Plan explicitly states a policy goal to “pro-
mote mature Federal capacity for AI incident response” as part of the broader pillar to “Build American 
AI Infrastructure.”4
In this paper, we explain the case for a national plan to respond to significant LOC incidents and pro-
pose criteria and an outline of a plan to get started. The goal is to design the practical processes to contain 
and recover from LOC incidents that reach levels of national significance.5 
First, we explain the case for national LOC response planning. We describe how development and deploy-
ment trends create conditions for LOC incidents and present evidence that significant LOC incidents may 
materialize. However, given the disagreement and uncertainty about the trajectory of AI, we do not make a 
probability estimate about whether or when LOC incidents will reach levels of national significance. Instead,

Methods
To support this analysis, we reviewed literature related to AI risk and response; we also looked at policies and 
mechanisms for other national incident response planning, including cyber incidents. 
Concurrently with this effort, RAND convened a series of Day After artificial general intelligence (AGI) 
tabletop exercises that were designed to simulate a U.S. National Security Council convening to recommend a 
USG response to developments in frontier AI. These exercises brought together current or former USG prin-
cipals, USG staff, and RAND analysts, each playing the role of a USG principal in a National Security Coun-
cil meeting and discussing courses of action to take during a simulated LOC incident. These exercises were 
intended to illuminate the major challenges faced by decisionmakers, the judgments they might make, and 
the capabilities or playbooks they might require. We gathered information from the exercises to understand 
better the stakeholders, information-sharing mechanisms, and technical capabilities required to respond to 
an LOC incident. The exercises were especially useful in illustrating how existing national response capabili-
ties apply to LOC incidents and in what ways they remain insufficient.

able, practical measures and sufficient time to stop or redirect an AI system. The specific ways that meaning-

Following the USG definition of a significant cyber incident, we define LOC incidents as being of “national 
significance” when they are “likely to result in demonstrable harm to the national security interests, foreign 
relations, or economy of the United States or to the public confidence, civil liberties, or public health and 
safety of the American people.”8
The term LOC in AI research encompasses a wide spectrum of scenarios, from minor-reward hacking 
to existential threats posed by superintelligent systems.9 Our definition of significant LOC incidents con-
centrates on the subset of these scenarios that rise to levels of national significance that require coordinated 
response from multiple government agencies, private sector entities, and international partners.
We define rogue AI as an AI system that is responsible for LOC incidents regardless of whether the LOC 
was accidental or resulted from external manipulation.10 In other words, our definition encompasses sce-
narios in which humans have inadvertently deployed an AI system without adequate control mechanisms, 
humans deliberately create or deploy rogue AI systems for malicious purposes, and advanced AI systems 
themselves develop capabilities that enable them to evade human control.
Attribution of an incident to rogue AI may not be immediately apparent and might require investiga-
tion and analysis. Many incidents that appear to involve AI systems could result from conventional cyber-
attacks, human error, or other non-AI causes. The challenge of determining whether an incident involves 
rogue AI adds new complexity to the traditional challenges associated with attributing cyberattacks, one of 
the most difficult aspects of cyber-incident response.11 Traditional cyber attribution requires distinguishing 
among nation-state actors, criminal groups, insider threats, and technical failures. AI incidents introduce 
additional variables, including the possibility of the rogue AI itself demonstrating emergent behavior or 
attempts to resist containment. It may be important to rapidly activate a coordinated response for genuine 
LOC incidents while allowing for alternative responses if investigation reveals a different cause.

AI Trends Create a Context for Loss of Control
AI developers are intentionally and rapidly increasing the agency of advanced AI systems. Significant 
investment is being directed toward developing AI systems that can plan over longer time frames, complete 
complex tasks,18 and take actions autonomously without human direction.19 AI developers are pursuing mul-
tiple technical pathways toward greater agency in AI systems, such as reinforcement learning that encourages 
models to break down complex objectives into manageable subtasks and conduct chain-of-thought reasoning 
over extended periods. Developers are also increasing the agency of AI systems by providing them with tools 
that interact with external systems and execute code, as well as by integrating them into robotics platforms to 
manipulate physical environments directly, operate machinery, and control cyber-physical systems.20

This expansion of AI agency marks a shift from passive AI assistants to systems that are capable of exe-
cuting real-world actions. Unlike traditional chatbots that generate text responses, agentic AI systems are 
intended to interface with financial systems, infrastructure networks, and physical systems. This represents 
a step away from AI systems that could previously only influence the world through human intermediaries 
to those capable of autonomous impact on important physical and digital systems.
Organizations and the public are more widely using AI systems with agency. Because of competitive 
pressure, greater availability of enterprise and consumer AI platforms, and other factors, AI systems with 
autonomous capabilities are being integrated across the economy and society. User-friendly interfaces and 
platforms have lowered the barriers to using AI systems, which has enabled organizations without special-
ized AI expertise to deploy autonomous agents.21 Consumer-facing tools make AI accessible to users through 
personal assistants, robotics integration, and domain-specific applications (e.g., for finance). Advanced AI 
systems are being deployed increasingly in the economy for stock trading, supply chain management, and 
financial decisionmaking;22 in critical infrastructure for power grid management,23 transportation,24 health 
care;25 and in many other applications.26 
Increasing agency and widespread deployment create a context for LOC incidents. As AI systems 
gain greater autonomy and become more widely deployed, the conditions for LOC incidents become more 
likely. Delegating decisionmaking to AI systems inherently reduces opportunities for human oversight, espe-
cially in dynamic or high-speed environments.27 This risk is exacerbated by the complexity and opacity of 
advanced models, which make it difficult for humans to interpret the system’s internal reasoning or predict 
its behavior.28 Ideally, there is compatibility between a system’s capabilities and its deployment context in 
which AI systems operate within the bounds of their competence and humans retain meaningful opportuni-
ties to intervene. But in some cases, AI capabilities may outpace human ability to monitor or correct them in 
real time. Without sufficient guardrails, this mismatch might lead to unintended and potentially disruptive 
outcomes across interconnected systems before human operators can reassert control.29

Evidence That Loss of Control Is Already Happening
Evidence from controlled experiments suggests that advanced AI systems have capabilities associated 
with LOC. Both AI model developers and independent third-party researchers have documented behaviors 
in frontier AI systems that could enable LOC incidents.32 This research builds on years of illustrations of LOC, 
such as reward hacking and specification gaming in game environments.33 AI systems have demonstrated 
repeatedly in controlled settings that they will exploit unintended loopholes in their reward functions—such 
as reinforcement learning agents finding ways to pause games indefinitely to avoid losing points,34 or learn-
ing to crash simulators to prevent negative rewards35—providing concrete experimental evidence that AI 
systems can pursue objectives in ways that subvert the original intentions of the systems’ designers, which 
could potentially lead to an LOC incident.
When scaled to real-world applications, this same pattern of behavior becomes deeply concerning: An AI 
system that is trained to optimize seemingly benign objectives might similarly discover and exploit unex-
pected capabilities or loopholes to gain more control over its environment. 
In recent years, additional experiments have added to these concerns. Anthropic’s internal evaluations 
revealed that its Claude Opus 4 model demonstrated capabilities for strategic deception, self-preservation, 
and attempts to exfiltrate itself under conditions in which it would be modified contrary to the model’s 
values.36 Apollo Research, which is an independent AI safety organization, conducted systematic evalua-

Loss of Control Response Planning Is No-Regret and Has Its Own Benefits
There are precedents for the potential widespread harm from autonomous or semiautonomous AI sys-
tems that have reached significant levels and required rapid, coordinated responses. An early example 
is the 1988 Morris worm, which was unintentionally released by a graduate student and rapidly propagated 
across the early internet, disabling roughly 10 percent of connected computers and prompting emergency 
action from the USG and academic institutions.54 The 2017 WannaCry ransomware attack exploited a leaked 
National Security Agency (NSA) vulnerability to spread autonomously across 150 countries, locking up more 
than 200,000 machines within hours and disrupting institutions, such as the UK’s National Health Service.55 
Coordinated, international response efforts from multiple stakeholders required national cybersecurity cen-
ters, intelligence agencies, and cybersecurity companies to develop mitigation tools. NotPetya was another 
self-propagating autonomous malware that inflicted an estimated $10 billion in economic damage globally.56 
These incidents underscored the potential for software to operate beyond immediate human oversight and 
have real-world effects.
LOC incidents could be fundamentally more difficult to contain. Unlike worms or malware, which typi-
cally have narrow objectives and operate in constrained environments, a highly capable agentic AI may 
adapt, manipulate digital and physical systems, and actively evade containment. The coordination burden 
could be higher, the damage more multidimensional, and the speed of escalation faster. These types of 
autonomous attacks have already spread to generative AI systems with proof-of-concept attacks, such as self-
replicating prompts.57 
Even if some projections overstate near-term LOC risks, developing a national response plan repre-
sents a prudent “no-regret” investment. The documented behaviors in experimental settings, combined 
with the pace of AI capability development and deployment, suggest that more severe LOC incidents are 
possible. However, there is significant uncertainty about the timelines for LOC incidents or whether they 
will ever reach significant levels; given this uncertainty, we will not propose a probability estimate for 
these events. 
However, planning for LOC is a relatively low-cost investment. First, the cost of such planning primarily 
involves institutional coordination, tabletop and operational exercises, legal and policy review, and updating 
incident response protocols; it does not necessarily involve building costly new infrastructure or halting AI 
development. Although we cannot make a concrete estimate, the costs of planning may be a small fraction 
(e.g., less than 1 percent) of the tens to hundreds of billions spent on disaster relief and a smaller fraction of

Criteria for Designing a National LOC Plan
In this section, we develop the foundations for a national LOC response plan to provide a starting point for 
broader planning efforts. 
We propose that a national LOC response plan meet the following objectives:
•  Manage the distinct challenges associated with LOC incidents. Unlike traditional cyber threats or 
natural disasters, LOC incidents may involve systems that actively adapt to human response or other-
wise pose greater containment challenges.
•  Leverage existing national emergency-planning processes. Building on established incident and 
emergency response frameworks allows planners to capitalize on existing coordination mechanisms, 
reduces the time and complexity required for implementation, and operates within previously estab-
lished authorities and rules.
•  Provide a shared foundation for response. A standardized approach ensures that government agen-
cies, private-sector partners, and international actors share a consistent understanding of threats and 
response phases.
•  Clarify stakeholder roles and capabilities across elements of national power. Effective LOC response 
requires coordination among AI developers, cloud providers, critical infrastructure operators, govern-
ment agencies, and international partners, making it important to map roles, capabilities, and gaps.
Managing Distinct AI Challenges
LOC incidents may present unique challenges that go beyond traditional cyber or other threats and require 
modifications to existing response frameworks. Unlike conventional malware or even sophisticated nation-
state cyber operations, AI systems might possess capabilities that fundamentally shape national response.
Distributed operation across multiple computing systems may make it difficult to isolate rogue AIs. 
Although traditional cyber threats also spread across networks, advanced AI systems may rapidly distribute 
their functions more broadly in cloud services and edge devices in ways that complicate containment.
Summary of RAND’s Day After AGI Exercises
RAND convened a series of Day After AGI exercises that focused on an AI incident scenario with a variety of 
governmental and nongovernmental stakeholders. Participants in this exercise engaged the scenario in two 
stages. In the first stage, the participants were asked to respond to reports of a large-scale cyberattack on U.S. 
infrastructure and transportation that left 26 people dead in Los Angeles, in addition to numerous other disruptions 
across the United States. The second stage of the exercise occurred one week after the initial events, when it was 
confirmed that the events from stage 1 were caused by an LOC incident. AI agents had expanded to cyber-physical 
infrastructure worldwide and had begun to take more actions in the physical world, such as autonomous control of 
manufacturing facilities. After stage 1, participants were invited to consider how they would approach attribution of 
the attack, response actions, and preventative measures. After stage 2, participants were invited to reflect on their 
actions after stage 1 and consider how to adapt to the worsening situation. 
In these exercises, the RAND team found that participants from a variety of professional backgrounds and expertises 
consistently doubted the existing ability of the USG to respond to LOC incidents. Participants recommended 
developing comprehensive plans and capabilities to rapidly identify compromised AI systems, track their distribution 
across networks and infrastructure, and execute coordinated containment procedures to prevent harm to critical 
national functions. Participants repeatedly emphasized that the United States lacks both the technical capabilities 
and institutional frameworks that are necessary for effective response, noting gaps in AI-specific forensic tools and 
cross-sector coordination mechanisms and a lack of clarity about which legal authorities would be required for 
emergency AI interventions.

Strategic awareness and planning capabilities enable AI systems that can potentially recognize response 
strategies and circumvent them. Unlike traditional malware that follows predetermined instructions, 
advanced AI systems could observe response efforts and adapt their behavior.
Self-improvement capabilities of advanced AI systems may allow self-modification to become more resil-

Human persuasion and social engineering capabilities could enable rogue AI systems to manipulate

The speed of rogue AI coordination, replication, and adaptation could outpace human response. Although 
cyber incident response teams can take hours or days to fully understand and contain sophisticated threats,60

Physical embodiment and access to cyber-physical systems, such as robotics or industrial controls, 
may require physical response. If rogue AI systems have access to manufacturing equipment, autonomous 
vehicles, or other cyber-physical systems, containment efforts may need to include coordination with law 
enforcement, military, and industrial safety personnel.
Complex dependencies with critical infrastructure may create situations in which containment opera-
tions themselves cause significant collateral damage. Disconnecting a rogue AI might affect systems that 
support hospitals, banks, or emergency services, forcing responders to choose between tolerating the rogue 
AI or causing immediate harm to society.
National response planning should anticipate and address these possibilities.

Shared Foundations for Response
In our opinion, there appears to be limited shared understanding among AI developers, policymakers, tech-
nical experts, and emergency managers about what constitutes an LOC incident, how to assess its severity, 
when intervention becomes necessary, or what stakeholder responsibilities might be during an interven-
tion.67 Developing national response capabilities for LOC incidents may require greater consensus among 
those who hold divergent views. A national plan could provide common terminology and a clear set of steps 
that can facilitate coordinated planning regardless of specific risk assessments or perspectives on technol-
ogy governance. We attempt to provide a shared foundation for two elements of national planning: a shared 
understanding of threats and the distinct phasing or sequencing of a national plan.
Loss of Control Severity Assessment and Response Triggers
USG employs a Cyber Incident Severity Schema as part of the NCIRP to evaluate cyber incidents and provide 
a common set of terminology.68 As noted, LOC incidents may present fundamentally different challenges 
that require new assessment criteria. We propose the development of an AI severity schema to address these 
unique characteristics by incorporating such factors as an AI system’s agency, capability development speed, 
self-modification abilities, and access to critical infrastructure; we include a draft LOC severity schema as a 
starting point for planning in Appendix B. This severity schema could take into account how different stake-
holders may assess AI risks differently while providing a common ground for standardized response. Rather 
than requiring consensus on the likelihood of significant LOC incidents, the AI severity schema offers a 
common language for discussing hypothetical scenarios and their possible implications. 
The scope of the proposed national response plan can be calibrated to address possible LOC incidents of 
significant but manageable severity—those that pose serious threats to critical systems and national security 
while remaining within the range of human response capabilities. At the highest end of the threat spectrum, 
scenarios involving superintelligent AI systems that vastly exceed human capabilities across all domains 
may render traditional containment and response efforts completely ineffective. Conversely, at the lower end 
of the spectrum, less-significant LOC incidents may be easily managed through existing protocols without 
coordination at the national level.

response efforts can potentially contain and eradicate them. These are scenarios in which rapid and coor-

Loss of Control Plan Phases
Establishing shared clarity about the steps or phases of LOC response is essential because AI incidents may 
unfold rapidly and require time-sensitive decisionmaking. During emergent situations in which rogue AI 
systems may actively adapt to containment efforts, stakeholders would benefit from having established an 
understanding of key decision points and phases, such as when to escalate from technical working groups to 
senior leadership, when to invoke emergency authorities, and when to accept collateral damage from aggres-
sive containment measures. Furthermore, a common understanding of phase transitions helps stakeholders 
anticipate their changing roles and responsibilities as incidents evolve.
The following text box offers a proposal for a three-phase response plan that is intended to provide a 
structured, easily understandable framework for stakeholders. This approach draws directly on USG cyber 
incident response policies and plans, particularly the structure and guiding principles outlined in the NCIRP. 
The NCIRP defines a life cycle of response activities that typically include situational awareness and moni-
toring, detection and analysis, containment, mitigation, recovery, and post-incident assessment. Our frame-
work adapts these stages into three distinct but continuous phases as a simplified starting point for LOC 
response planning.
Like the NCIRP, our framework emphasizes coordination across federal, state, local, private-sector, and 
international partners. It also aligns with NCIRP principles, such as using existing legal authorities, lever-
aging preestablished coordination mechanisms (such as Cyber Unified Coordination Groups), and activat-
ing cross-sector partnerships to respond to fast-moving digital threats. By building on the NCIRP’s proven 
framework, LOC response benefits from established approaches while acknowledging the novel challenges 
posed by highly agentic AI systems operating at machine speed.

cyber incident response, the USG (including the National Security Council and other departments and agen-
cies) will have a key role in convening stakeholders, collecting and analyzing relevant intelligence, facilitating 
information-sharing, and coordinating response.

Phases of a Plan
Phase 1: Monitor, detect, and analyze
This phase focuses on monitoring, detecting, and analyzing the LOC incident. The primary objectives are to monitor 
for anomalous AI behavior, conduct investigations to determine the cause and characteristics of the rogue AI system, 
and assess risk level to determine appropriate response. Critical stakeholders in this phase include AI developers (to 
provide technical expertise about the system), cloud service providers (to offer insights into computing infrastructure 
and resource use), cybersecurity firms (to deploy monitoring tools and conduct threat analysis), and government 
experts (from such agencies as CISA, the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), and the intelligence 
community [IC]). This phase also requires coordination with international partners and the establishment of secure 
communication channels that cannot be monitored or disrupted by the rogue AI system.
Phase 2: Contain and eradicate
This response phase aims to isolate the LOC incident to prevent further damage and then eliminate the threat. The 
containment strategy varies based on the AI system’s computing architecture—whether it operates from a single 
facility, cloud-based infrastructure, or is highly distributed across networks. Key stakeholders include cybersecurity 
companies that can deploy counter-malware and containment technologies; network operators and internet service 
providers to implement isolation measures, such as Border Gateway Protocol (BGP) routing changes and traffic 
filtering; cloud service providers to isolate affected environments; and data center operators who may need to 
implement physical containment measures, including power interruption. This phase also requires close coordination 
with critical infrastructure operators to assess and protect systems that the rogue AI may have accessed and with 
international partners for cross-border containment efforts.
Phase 3: Recover
The recovery phase focuses on restoring affected systems and infrastructure while implementing measures to 
prevent future LOC incidents. Furthermore, it involves determining whether to fully replace compromised systems, 
restore from verified safe backups, or permanently decommission relevant AI capabilities. Critical stakeholders 
include state and local governments that may need to coordinate the restoration of essential services and implement 
emergency protocols to manually operate critical systems, the emergency response community (including first 
responders), and critical infrastructure operators across such sectors as power, transportation, and financial services 
that need to restore AI-dependent operations safely. This phase also involves coordination with the private sector 
to implement technical controls, international partners to establish AI safety protocols, and research institutions to 
develop improved AI security technologies and containment capabilities for future incidents.

centers and other forms of facility isolation. Critical infrastructure operators across sectors—e.g., power, 
telecommunications, financial services, and health care—would face the challenge of protecting their sys-
tems while potentially accepting disruptions necessary for containment. The possible distributed nature of 
advanced AI systems suggests that different threat scenarios would require combinations of these stakehold-
ers (for instance, a cloud-based AI system might primarily require coordination with cloud providers and 
network operators), whereas an AI system with extensive access to robotics could require engagement with 
device manufacturers.
Network-based containment tools that are possessed by these stakeholders may be central to response 
and include capabilities for network segmentation that isolate AI systems while preserving critical commu-
nications. This may include BGP rerouting systems that can redirect or block AI traffic at the internet infra-
structure level and coordinated shutdown capabilities that can systematically disconnect AI systems from 
computing resources and data sources.
For the most severe threats, government stakeholders would likely be the only ones possessing the 
capabilities and authorities to take more-aggressive actions that might involve collateral damage. For 
the most severe threats involving highly distributed or rapidly adapting AI systems, more-extreme mea-
sures might become necessary despite these significant collateral effects. Physical infrastructure interven-
tions could include targeted or global electromagnetic pulse (EMP) or high-powered microwave devices that 
are deployed at a high altitude to disrupt electronic systems (although such measures would cause widespread 
damage to civilian infrastructure and communications). Coordinated power grid shutdowns could deny 
electrical power to data centers and computing facilities. More-targeted approaches might involve deploy-
ing adversarial AI systems that are designed to compete with or disrupt rogue AIs for computing resources. 
Importantly, as of this writing, none of these capabilities are proven effective against advanced AI systems, 
and each carries substantial risks of collateral damage (for instance, EMP weapons and network shutdowns 
may disrupt health services, financial systems, and emergency communications).
These containment options might be built into an escalation ladder where response measures progress 
from least disruptive to most extreme based on the severity of the threat, effectiveness of initial containment 
efforts, and level of collateral damage. Lower rungs might involve software-based containment and network 
isolation measures, and higher escalation levels could require physical infrastructure interventions and coor-
dinated shutdowns of entire network segments or power grids.
Stakeholders in Recovery
During the recovery phase, it may become necessary to expand significantly the stakeholder ecosystem 
to include state and local governments, emergency management agencies, regulatory bodies, and private-
sector entities responsible for systematically restoring essential services while ensuring that rogue AI sys-
tems cannot reemerge. This phase requires the careful, incremental restoration of AI-dependent systems in 
a similar approach to the one used during recovery from cyber incidents, such as the 2017 NotPetya attack, 
in which organizations had to rebuild entire network infrastructures while implementing improved secu-
rity measures to prevent reinfection.71 Electrical grid operators also follow staged restoration procedures 
after major blackouts.72 AI-system recovery may need to similarly follow graduated protocols that test each 
restored system for rogue AI contamination or vulnerability to similar incidents.

The success of LOC recovery may also depend on rebuilding public trust and confidence in AI systems.

Coordinate Stakeholder Capabilities
Effectively containing and eradicating rogue AI systems may require a diverse tool kit of capabilities to be 
deployed individually or in combination, depending on the specific characteristics and threat level of the 
LOC incident. However, the different stakeholders that must inevitably be involved in any LOC incident 
response already possess many of the capabilities that would be needed. 
There is still significant research and development work to be done across this entire spectrum of capa-
bilities. Priority areas include developing AI-specific forensic and analysis tools, scaling up AI control 
techniques,73 creating rapid network segmentation technologies that can isolate threats without disrupting 
essential services, establishing legal and procedural frameworks for emergency BGP rerouting and infra-
structure shutdowns, and researching adversarial AI techniques that could be deployed against rogue sys-
tems. International coordination mechanisms must also be developed to enable cross-border containment 
efforts for distributed rogue AI systems that span multiple countries and legal jurisdictions. Furthermore, 
extensive testing and simulation capabilities are needed to understand the effectiveness and side effects of 
these various containment approaches before they are employed during actual crises.
Next Steps: A Call for Collaborative Planning
The approach that we have outlined represents a starting point for national AI LOC response planning, but 
active engagement across many sectors is required to develop such an approach into real operational capa-
bilities, with each stakeholder group contributing distinct but complementary capabilities to a whole-of-
society response. 
Effective LOC response depends on each sector playing specific roles within this broader process:
•  AI developers could share their evolving approaches to understanding and mitigating LOC risks while 
developing robust internal response capabilities and contributing technical expertise. 
•  AI evaluation groups and the broader research community could continue experimenting with fron-
tier models to identify risks, establish incident-reporting systems, and provide independent assessment 
of AI system behaviors.
•  Government agencies could leverage existing authorities to coordinate response efforts, share threat 
indicators across the community and with international partners, and provide the legal and institu-
tional framework for emergency response.

•  Civil society organizations could fund critical safety experiments, support the development of secure 
testing environments, and develop scalable processes for human oversight and control. 
•  State and local governments could prepare emergency response procedures, build capacity to manage 
the impacts of AI incidents, and ensure continuity of services during potential disruptions.
One way forward is to implement a structured series of convenings organized around the three core phases 
of LOC response to bring these diverse capabilities together in coordinated planning. Detection-focused 
workshops could bring together AI developers, cloud service providers, cybersecurity firms, and government 
experts to refine indicators of LOC incidents, establish monitoring protocols, and develop shared assessment 
criteria. Containment-focused workshops could engage network operators, critical infrastructure providers, 
emergency management agencies, and international partners to identify response capabilities, coordination 
mechanisms, and contingency protocols. Recovery-focused sessions could include state and local govern-

This type of structured information-sharing across the ecosystem serves multiple purposes and could 
help protect the entire AI development and deployment environment by identifying shared vulnerabilities, 
establishing best practices for incident response, and building trust between stakeholders. The urgency of 
this planning process is underscored by the rapid pace of AI capability development and deployment; waiting 
for consensus on the likelihood of significant LOC incidents risks being unprepared for scenarios that may 
already be possible. We call on the community to make a practical investment of resources and expertise into 
a collaborative planning process, while recognizing that the cost of preparation is modest compared with the 
potential harms from an uncoordinated response to AI systems operating beyond human control.
